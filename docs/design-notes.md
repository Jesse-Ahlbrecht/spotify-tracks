# Design notes

The durable reasoning behind the project: why the data, the analysis and the app are shaped the
way they are. Results live in [findings.md](findings.md); how to run things is in the
[README](../README.md).

## Data sources

- **Dataset A** `zaheenhamidani/ultimate-spotify-tracks-db`: genre labels and audio features, no
  year. Used for the genre and popularity realms. It labels some tracks under several genres, which
  is itself evidence of how fuzzy genre is.
- **Dataset B** `yamaerenay/spotify-dataset-19212020-600k-tracks`: has `year`. Used for every
  time-based realm and the app.
- **US economy and sentiment** from FRED: unemployment (`UNRATE`), CPI (`CPIAUCNS`, turned into
  year-on-year inflation) and University of Michigan consumer sentiment (`UMCSENT`).

Everything downloads without credentials: Kaggle's
`GET https://www.kaggle.com/api/v1/datasets/download/<owner>/<slug>` returns a real zip, and FRED's
`fredgraph.csv` is public. `scripts/download_data.sh` fetches it all (raw data is git-ignored).

## Data caveats

- **Early decades are thin and unrepresentative.** The 1920s–40s are about 6.6% of dataset B.
  What survives on Spotify from that era is a reissue sample, heavy on classical and jazz, with
  about a third of tracks carrying no artist genre tag. Its features are computed on low-fidelity
  mono transfers, which push `acousticness` up and `energy`/`loudness` down for technical rather
  than artistic reasons. Pre-1950 points stay in the trend lines, but no headline number rests on
  them alone. Rigorous comparisons start around 1950–60.
- **Genre for dataset B is a join.** `attach_genre()` maps each track's first artist to that
  artist's Spotify genres (`artists.csv`), then to a coarse supergenre. About 72% of tracks land
  in a named bucket; the rest are "other". This is what makes the within-genre / between-genre
  decomposition of the valence decline possible.
- **Everything is correlational.** Trends over time are often one-way drift driven by recording
  technology, not cycles, and nothing here establishes cause.
- **Methods note: the retracted "escapism" signal.** A first pass found detrended valence
  correlated about +0.31 with the misery index ("brighter music in hard times"). Varying the start
  year showed it came from the volatile late-1940s catalog; from 1960 on it is about zero. The
  notebook now drops years with fewer than 200 tracks and reports the null. Any new correlation
  against a yearly series should get the same start-year check.
- **`popularity` is a present-day snapshot.** It favours recent music and biases anything built on
  it (hit islands, representative tracks) toward today's taste.

## Architecture

Python computes; the browser only renders. `analysis/common.py` holds the loaders and cleaning
used by both the notebooks and `scripts/export_app_data.py`, so the app's numbers match the
notebooks. The export does all derivation (per-decade shares, representative-track selection, the
economy join and z-scoring) and writes about 18 KB of JSON to `app/public/data/`. The 587k-row
dataset never reaches the browser. The notebooks are generated by `scripts/build_notebooks.py`
and executed with nbconvert; edit the builder, not the `.ipynb`. The app is Vite + React in plain
JSX with hand-written SVG charts (`d3-scale`/`d3-shape`, no chart library); Playwright is a dev
dependency for the layout checks only.

## Charts

- **Shares of songs, not scores.** An earlier version plotted each story's decade averages as a
  path across two Spotify features. Readers had to trace the path to see time, the axes were
  zoomed to tiny ranges (average valence spans only 0.50–0.60 over the century), and 0–1 feature
  scores mean nothing to most readers. Each story now shows two bar charts, one bar per decade on a
  fixed 0–100% scale, each counting the share of songs that pass a plain yes/no test (valence ≥ 0.5
  "sounds happy", acousticness < 0.5 "sounds electric", mode = minor, and so on). 0.5 is the middle
  of Spotify's scale; it is a presentation threshold, not a finding, and the story intro prints
  each rule.
- **Bars fill as you travel.** The current decade is full colour with its value printed, earlier
  decades are lighter, later ones are empty slots; a 50% line is the only reference. Captions never
  quote Spotify's 0–1 numbers, only shape, direction or proportions of songs.
- **One axis only.** The economy coda (Mood story only) z-scores valence, misery and sentiment onto
  one axis rather than using a dual axis.
- **Phones get their own geometry, not a scaled-down chart.** An SVG sized by its viewBox shrinks its
  text with it, so each phone deck page draws its own chart, with its own decade active, in fixed
  `compact` / `compactTall` geometries where one user unit is about one CSS pixel. On desktop the
  single sticky chart measures its card and uses that size as its viewBox.

## Layouts

- **Desktop full journey:** a sticky chart and players beside a column of decade captions.
- **Phone full journey:** a different component tree, not the desktop one reflowed. Each decade is
  one full-screen page that moves as a whole: its own compact chart, the decade, the caption, its
  songs in a sideways carousel, and a Next button. An earlier version pinned one chart above the
  pages, which kept the top of the screen still while only the lower half scrolled. The breakpoint
  includes short landscape screens, which are wider than 900px but too short for the desktop layout.
- **Quick version:** a full-screen feed, one card per decade, with a headline, one line of story
  and one song, and no charts. On a phone each card has a Next button and a column of dots down the
  right edge shows progress; on desktop there are ↑/↓ arrows and a decade timeline.
- **The address keeps the place.** The story (when not the first) and, while the quick version is
  open, its card live in the query string (`?story=beat&quick=3`, via `replaceState`, so no history
  entries), and a refresh returns to them. Without it a refresh reopened the first story's long
  page at the scroll position the browser restored.

## Scrolling

- **Input is never intercepted on the long page.** An earlier wheel handler (one event, one decade,
  `preventDefault()`) suited a mouse but discarded trackpad momentum and did nothing for touch, so
  all listeners are passive.
- **No scroll-snap on the desktop page.** Its decade steps are shorter than the viewport, and
  Chromium re-snaps after every discrete wheel event, so wheel and trackpad input was dragged back
  to where it started. Instead `useSettleToStep` waits until scrolling goes quiet (100 ms, or 40 ms
  once it is already slow) and glides to a decade. It settles only in the direction of travel
  (settling to the nearest decade pinned mouse users in place), and only when the reader is not
  already parked on a decade. Any new input cancels a glide, and any deliberate jump (arrows,
  "Dive in", jump to top) cancels a settle in progress.
- **Mandatory snap where every page is a screen.** The phone deck and the quick version use
  `scroll-snap-type: y mandatory` with `scroll-snap-stop: always`, so one swipe is one decade. This
  is the case snap is built for. On the deck, every full-height block (hero, intro, decades, coda,
  footer) must be a snap point, or mandatory snap pulls the reader away from it. The settle glide is
  off on phones so that only one mechanism steers the scroll, and the decade arrows are desktop
  only: on a phone you swipe or tap Next.
- **Jumping to the top under mandatory snap.** iOS Safari snaps a programmatic jump to the top
  straight back to the page it left, so `scrollToTop` lifts `scroll-snap-type` while the page lands,
  instantly, and then restores it.
- `app/scripts/check-scroll.mjs` (`npm run check:scroll`) drives real wheel and touch input in
  Playwright at desktop and several phone sizes. Each failure above is a named regression check.
  The checks are timing-sensitive and run in emulation; they are necessary, not sufficient.

## Representative tracks

For each story and decade, `export_app_data.py` takes the decade's 100 most popular tracks and
picks the 3 nearest the decade's centroid in the z-scored space of that story's two features (for
"Two kinds of sad", valence and the per-track minor-key flag). The aim is songs that are
recognisable and sound like the decade on that story's terms.

Known issue: ties in `popularity` make the top-100 cut order-dependent, so a re-export can pick
different songs. The committed `tracks.json` is the reference; a stable tie-break in
`pick_tracks` is needed before regenerating it.

Possible improvements: a diversity guard (distinct artists, no remasters or alternate versions);
blending popularity, normalised within the decade, with distance to the centroid instead of a hard
top-100 cut, so thin early decades are not over-filtered; one track at the centroid plus one or two
from the edges, to show spread; better recognisability for early decades, where present-day
popularity is a weak signal; and a "most central / most iconic" toggle on the same score.

## Spotify players

Players are Spotify embeds (`Player.jsx`), controlled through the `playback_update` / `ready`
messages they post to the page and the `play` / `pause` commands they accept.

- **How a song starts.** iOS Safari starts an embed that has never played only for a `play` posted
  synchronously inside a tap, click or key handler of the page. A `play` posted later (from an
  effect after the scroll lands, a timer, or in reply to the embed's `ready`) is ignored. Once an
  embed has played, it can be started again without a gesture.
- **So every tap, click or key navigation starts its song in the handler**: Start, Next, Dive in,
  the arrows, the timeline and the arrow keys all start the target decade's first song themselves
  (`playSong`, `playDecade`). The arrival effects (`useAutoplay`) remain for browsers that allow
  autoplay and for embeds that have already played; they skip an embed a handler asked within the
  last few seconds. `play` is never sent to an embed that is already playing or has not yet sent
  `ready` (it throws inside the embed); likewise `pause` goes only to embeds known to be playing.
- **Swipes cannot start a new song in Safari**, because a swipe is not a gesture it accepts, so a
  "Tap to play" button of the page's own covers the current player until that player has played.
  On iOS and iPadOS, where every browser is WebKit, it shows as soon as the card or page lands.
  Elsewhere it waits about 1.2 s for the browser to start the song by itself, and shows at once
  from then on if a song failed to start after the reader had already tapped or clicked. "Has
  played" belongs to the player, not the song: the same song in the other journey is another
  player and needs its own tap.
- **One song at a time.** Starting a song pauses the others. Moving on pauses what is playing: a
  new decade in the full journey, a new card in the quick version (including the start and end
  cards, which have no song), a change of story or mode, or leaving the journey for the hero,
  explainer, coda or footer, where no player is in view. On a decade or card change, a song a tap
  has just asked for is spared, because it may not have reported playing yet.
- **An embed that has played is never unmounted.** The embeds appear to share one playback session,
  and removing one that has played left every later one stuck buffering.
- **Rejected:** Spotify's iFrame API with one controller and `loadUri` (it reloads the player,
  which locks it again). Unlocking every embed up front by playing and pausing it (a pause sent while
  an embed is buffering is ignored, so songs overlapped, and Spotify covers a paused embed with its
  own "Get Spotify" banner).
- **Rendering.** Embeds use `?theme=0`, one neutral look instead of colours taken from album art.
  The player slot is dark in the embed's own colour and the iframe stays hidden until it has
  loaded, so loading shows a dark card rather than a white box. On desktop the player is drawn at
  80% with a transform; on touch screens it is full size, because iOS Safari lays the embed out at
  the unscaled width and a scaled player leaves a white strip.
- **Verified** in Chromium (Playwright) and in iOS 26.5 Safari in the simulator. Not tested on a
  physical handset; desktop Safari untested.
